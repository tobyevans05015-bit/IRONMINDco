Module 04

Trauma Explorer

Not every wound comes from a single catastrophic event. Some are built slowly, from a thousand small moments of not being seen.

What Trauma Actually Means

The word trauma comes from the Greek τραῦμα — meaning "wound." Originally a purely physical term for a piercing injury, it was later extended to describe a psychological wound: an experience that overwhelms the nervous system's capacity to process it in the moment, leaving a residue that keeps activating long after the danger has passed.

Trauma isn't defined by the size of the event. It's defined by whether the nervous system had the support to complete its response at the time. A "small" event without support can wound more deeply than a "big" one with it.

The Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system runs the threat response beneath conscious control — which is why willpower alone rarely resolves it.

The Four Responses
Fight

Mobilizing aggression to meet the threat head-on. Shows up later as irritability, control, or a hair-trigger temper.

Flight

Mobilizing escape. Shows up later as restlessness, overworking, or an inability to sit still with discomfort.

Freeze

Shutting down when neither fight nor flight is possible. Shows up later as numbness, dissociation, or chronic fatigue.

Fawn

Appeasing the threat to stay safe. Shows up later as people-pleasing and a lost sense of one's own needs.

Developmental Trauma & Attachment

Developmental trauma accumulates across childhood rather than arriving in one event — inconsistent caregiving, emotional neglect, or an environment where a child's needs were routinely deprioritized. This shapes attachment style: the largely unconscious blueprint for how safe closeness feels in adulthood (secure, anxious, avoidant, or disorganized).

Core Wounds & Body Memory

A core wound is the earliest, most formative version of a recurring pain — abandonment, rejection, betrayal, injustice. The body often remembers what the conscious mind has filed away: chronic tension in the jaw, chest, or gut can be somatic memory long after the narrative memory has faded. See Body Scan for a guided version of this.
